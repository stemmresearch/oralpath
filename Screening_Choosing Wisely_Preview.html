Screening and Early Detection of Oral Cancer
Choosing Wisely

Why "detecting earlier" is not the same as "saving lives" — and what a rational approach to oral cancer detection actually looks like.

by Nicola Cirillo · 14 chapters, 160 pages

Despite decades of investment in early diagnosis, global oral cancer mortality has barely moved.

This book examines the paradox at the heart of oral cancer screening: more diagnoses, more surveillance, more technology — and yet the number of lives saved remains disappointingly small. Across fourteen chapters, it revisits the assumptions behind the "precursor-lesion paradigm" and discusses a risk-based framework for clinical practice.

Rational screening is a system, not a single test.

Chapter by chapter

The take-home messages

1 Essentials of Oral Cancer Epidemiology

  • Oral cancer is not a single epidemiological entity. Incidence, mortality and risk-factor profiles differ dramatically between world regions (South Asia vs. Europe vs. Sub-Saharan Africa), so any "global" screening recommendation ignores the fact that the disease looks different in different populations.

  • Despite decades of prevention and early-diagnosis efforts, global mortality has changed remarkably little. This is the paradox the whole book unpacks: more diagnoses, more detection technology, similar death rates.

  • Understanding the epidemiology is the prerequisite for any screening argument. You cannot rationally decide whom to screen, how often, or with which test until you know the baseline risk, incidence and mortality of the specific population you are considering.

2 Precursor lesions, conditions, and disorders

  • The OPMD label is heterogeneous by design. It groups together lesions (leukoplakia, erythroplakia), conditions (oral submucous fibrosis, lichen planus) and systemic disorders (Fanconi anaemia, dyskeratosis congenita) whose only common feature is a statistically elevated cancer risk — nothing else about them is biologically comparable.

  • Evidence for inclusion in the OPMD list is uneven and largely consensus-based. Some entities have robust prospective transformation data; others are included on the strength of expert opinion or a handful of case series.

  • The distinction between "lesion" (visible clinical abnormality) and "condition" (systemic predisposition) remains clinically useful even if it has fallen out of favour in recent classifications — because the two require completely different surveillance strategies.

3 How many oral cancers are preceded by clinically identifiable lesions?

  • Most oral cancers do not arise from a previously recognized OPMD. Population-based studies show only a minority (roughly 20% or less) of OSCCs had a clinically identifiable precursor lesion documented before diagnosis.

  • This finding directly contradicts the traditional "precursor-lesion paradigm" that underlies most screening strategies. If most cancers appear de novo, then screening for OPMDs will inevitably miss most future cancers regardless of how well it is done.

  • OPMD surveillance and OSCC screening are not the same thing and conflating them has led to misleading efficacy claims. A programme excellent at detecting OPMDs may still fail to reduce oral cancer mortality.

4 How many OPMDs will develop into oral cancers?

  • The vast majority of OPMDs will never become cancer. Annual malignant transformation rates for most OPMDs are below 1% — for leukoplakia, meta-analytic estimates cluster around 1–3% over the entire follow-up, not per year.

  • Labelling millions of people as OPMD patients requiring lifelong surveillance creates fertile ground for overdiagnosis and overtreatment — the harm side of an equation that is usually presented only from the benefit side.

  • Risk is not uniform within the OPMD category. Erythroplakia and dysplastic lesions carry substantially higher risk than homogeneous leukoplakia; treating them all with the same follow-up schedule wastes resources on the low-risk majority while potentially under-serving the high-risk minority.

5 A Novel Risk-Based Classification of Diseases with Oral Malignant Potential

  • Grouping heterogeneous diseases under one label produces heterogeneous — and often harmful — management. A rational classification must separate entities by magnitude and nature of the risk they carry, not by the historical fact that they were once described together.

  • Three tiers are proposed: oral precancerous diseases (OPD, high and demonstrable risk), oral potentially premalignant diseases (OPPD, uncertain or lower risk), and systemic conditions with oral malignant potential (rare genetic syndromes requiring tailored surveillance).

  • A risk-stratified framework aligns surveillance intensity with actual malignant potential, reducing the overdiagnosis burden on low-risk patients while concentrating clinical resources where the yield is highest.

6 Measuring the benefits of early detection of (oral) cancer

  • "More diagnoses" is not the same as "better outcomes." A screening programme can dramatically increase the number of cancers detected without moving mortality at all — the metric that actually matters for patients.

  • The only outcome that reliably measures screening benefit is a reduction in disease-specific mortality, ideally demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial. Everything else — earlier stage at diagnosis, higher 5-year survival, more lesions found — is a surrogate that can mislead.

  • The NHS-Galleri trial exemplifies the modern dilemma: massive investment in early detection technology whose real impact on mortality remains to be demonstrated, illustrating that even the most sophisticated programmes must ultimately prove their worth in lives saved.

7 Four misleading measures of success of early detection

  • Five-year survival is not evidence of screening benefit. It rises mechanically when screening detects cancers earlier (lead-time bias) or preferentially detects slow-growing tumours (length bias) — even when no life is actually prolonged.

  • Stage shift alone is insufficient proof of effectiveness. Detecting more early-stage cancers can look like success while the absolute number of late-stage cancers remains unchanged, meaning screening is finding additional early cancers (many indolent) rather than preventing progression to late stage.

  • Any success metric based on the diagnosed population rather than the whole target population is inherently biased in favour of the screening intervention. Mortality in the entire target population is the only fair yardstick.

8 Oral Cancer Screening: what the evidence shows

  • The Kerala cluster-randomized trial remains the only large RCT of oral cancer screening, and its overall mortality benefit was confined to a subgroup of high-risk users of tobacco and alcohol — not the general population.

  • No national health authority currently recommends population-wide oral cancer screening. WHO, USPSTF and equivalents converge on the same conclusion: insufficient evidence of benefit at population level.

  • Cost-effectiveness analyses that support screening typically depend on optimistic assumptions about OPMD progression and on ignoring the QALY loss from surveillance; sensitivity analyses that use realistic transformation rates make the case for screening much weaker.

9 How much harm from an OPMD diagnosis is enough to offset the potential benefits of oral cancer screening?

  • Benefit and burden fall on different people. Screening benefits the few whose cancer trajectory is actually altered; the burden of an OPMD diagnosis and lifelong surveillance falls on the many who would never have progressed.

  • The QALY loss required to cancel out screening benefits is very small. Modelling shows that even a modest, chronic reduction in quality of life among people living with an OPMD diagnosis is enough to make the net health effect of screening negative.

  • Existing economic evaluations appear favourable because they systematically underestimate the OPMD-surveillance burden — treating the OPMD state as if it were the healthy state — and overestimate malignant transformation rates. Correcting either assumption flips the conclusion.

10 From Screening to Practice

  • The rational alternative to population screening is opportunistic case-finding integrated into routine dental care, targeting adults who see a dentist for other reasons and enriching the yield with risk-based questions (tobacco, alcohol, betel, prior lesions).

  • General dentists are the true screening workforce. Their systematic visual examination of the oral cavity — under adequate light, with palpation — remains the highest-yield, lowest-cost detection tool available.

  • Adjuncts (autofluorescence, toluidine blue, brush cytology) and emerging AI-based image analysis should complement — not replace — clinical examination, and their added diagnostic value must be demonstrated in the low-prevalence primary-care setting, not just in tertiary referral series.

11 Biomarkers for Oral Disease Screening

  • The biomarker landscape for oral cancer is crowded but clinically underwhelming. Many candidates (salivary cytokines, miRNAs, ctDNA, methylation panels) discriminate cancer from health in research cohorts but fail the harder test of predicting malignant transformation of OPMDs.

  • Sample type matters strategically. Salivary biomarkers are attractive for non-invasive population screening; blood biomarkers suit systemic disease surveillance; tissue biomarkers add value at the biopsy stage but not for triage.

  • A biomarker validated for one purpose (e.g. distinguishing OSCC from normal mucosa) does not automatically qualify for another (e.g. screening asymptomatic populations). Confusing these use-cases has led to premature clinical translation of tools that are not fit for the intended purpose.

12 A Roadmap for Biomarker-Based Screening

  • Any useful screening biomarker must satisfy three orthogonal criteria: biomarker-dependent (validated surrogate, biologically meaningful, consistent across genders and ethnicities), test-dependent (safe, simple, cheap, sensitive, specific, with adequate predictive value), and disease-dependent (appropriate to the epidemiology of the target disease, with an effective treatment available).

  • Failure on any one axis disqualifies a biomarker for population screening, however impressive its performance on the other two — a lesson repeatedly ignored in commercial launches of oral cancer tests.

  • Predictive value depends on prevalence. Because oral cancer is rare in the general population, even a test with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity produces mostly false positives — mathematics that no clever molecular biology can overcome.

13 Reducing the harms of screening

  • The harms of screening are systematic and predictable, not incidental. They include false alarms, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, anxiety and diversion of resources from higher-yield interventions — and they scale with the intensity of screening applied to a low-prevalence disease.

  • Overestimation of individual risk drives both patient anxiety and clinician overreaction. Communicating absolute rather than relative risk, and time-framed rather than lifetime risk, is essential for shared decision-making that does not itself become a harm.

  • The most effective way to reduce screening harm is to screen only where evidence of net benefit exists — a much smaller domain than current practice suggests, especially for oral cancer.

14 From molecules to populations: an integrated view

  • Cancers do not have a single natural history. The traditional linear-progression model applies to only some tumours; others metastasize early, remain indolent, or regress — and any screening strategy that assumes one universal trajectory will misclassify large subgroups of patients.

  • The next model of oral cancer detection integrates education, risk-based classification, AI-assisted triage, molecular detection of existing cancer (not prediction of future cancer), real-time lesion mapping and population-level outcome evaluation — treating screening as a system, not a single test.

  • The research agenda should shift from "which OPMD will transform?" to "which lesion already harbours invasive disease?" — a more tractable question with immediate clinical utility, and one that reframes the whole biomarker enterprise around a realistic clinical need.
